Circadian rhythm is the body’s internal timing system, synchronized to the 24-hour day by light-dark cues detected in the retina and relayed to the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus. From the SCN, neural and hormonal signals coordinate peripheral clocks in organs and tissues, aligning physiological processes—including core body temperature, cortisol secretion, and melatonin release—with environmental cycles.

Sleep quality is tightly coupled to these oscillations. Melatonin, secreted by the pineal gland roughly two hours before habitual bedtime, lowers core body temperature and promotes sleep onset; its suppression by evening light exposure delays this cascade, pushing back sleep latency. Conversely, morning light triggers an SCN-mediated rise in cortisol and body temperature, consolidating arousal and setting the phase for the next circadian cycle. Misalignment—common in shift work, jet lag, or chronic evening screen use—flattens the amplitude of these rhythms, fragmenting slow-wave and REM sleep, reducing sleep efficiency, and increasing nocturnal arousals.

Genetic variants in clock genes (PER3, CRY1, CLOCK) modulate individual chronotype, influencing the intrinsic period length and sensitivity to light. Shorter alleles predispose to morningness and earlier melatonin onsets, whereas longer alleles delay the cycle, creating eveningness. Age-related changes in SCN neuronal firing and melatonin amplitude shift circadian phase in adolescence (later) and in older adults (earlier), altering sleep architecture independent of behavior.

Metabolic signals also feedback onto the SCN. Restricting food intake to the normal active phase reinforces circadian amplitude via nutrient-sensing pathways (SIRT1, AMPK), whereas nocturnal eating desynchronizes liver and muscle clocks from the central pacemaker, increasing stage N1 sleep and reducing REM density. Similarly, temperature rhythms—driven by circadian control of distal skin blood flow—gate the ability to fall and remain asleep; a delayed temperature nadir relative to desired bedtime correlates with lower sleep efficiency and higher wake-after-sleep-onset.

Thus, circadian integrity determines not only when sleep occurs but also its depth, continuity, and restorative value.
