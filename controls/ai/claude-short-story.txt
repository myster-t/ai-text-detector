The lighthouse keeper had not spoken to another person in forty-one days, and he was beginning to suspect that the gulls were talking about him.

Every morning he climbed the one hundred and twelve steps to the lamp room, polished the lens, and logged the weather in a ledger that no one would ever read. Every evening he climbed them again to light the lamp. In between, he mended nets he did not use, read the same three books in rotation, and watched the horizon for the supply boat that was now nine days late.

On the forty-second day, a rowboat appeared. It was not the supply boat. It was small and green and rowed by a girl who could not have been more than twelve, and she was rowing with the steady, uncomplaining rhythm of someone who had been at it for hours.

He met her at the landing. She shipped her oars, looked up at him, and said, "My grandmother says you have her book."

He did not know her grandmother. He said so.

"She says she lent it to the keeper here in 1961. A blue one, about birds. She wants it back before she dies, which the doctor says is Tuesday."

The keeper thought about the three books in his rotation. One of them was blue. One of them was about birds. He had always assumed it belonged to the lighthouse.

"Wait here," he said, and climbed the one hundred and twelve steps for the third time that day, faster than he had climbed them in years.
